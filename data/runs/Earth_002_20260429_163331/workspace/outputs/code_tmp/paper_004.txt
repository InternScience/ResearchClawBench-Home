Abstract12
Coastal ecosystems are recognized to provide a large range of servi ces including erosion13
prevention, clean water provision, or protection from tropical cy clones storms. However, with14
climate change, the increase in frequency and intensity of trop ical cyclones may alter the com-15
position of the ecosystems themselves potentially degradin g the various services they provide.16
While in general ecosystems are adapted to external perturbation s, extreme events can pro-17
mote rapid and durable shifts. In this study, we quantify the vuln erability of all ecosystems18
described as ecoregions at a global scale by analyzing the wind ﬁelds of tropical cyclones gen-19
erated from probabilistic historical tracks. We then investigate how tropical cyclone intensity20
and frequency shifting in a warming climate might lead to ecosyst em modiﬁcations. We show21
that a combined 13% of the surface of all terrestrial ecosystems is susceptible to transformation22
due to cyclone pattern changes between 2020 and 2050 under curre nt policies. Even for the23
most resilient ecosystems already experiencing winds above 60 m/s regularly, the average inter-24
val between two storms is projected to decrease from 19 to 12 years whi ch is potentially close25
to their recovery time. Our global probabilistic approach being embedded in the established26
IPCC climate risk frameworks enables the study of a broad temporal ra nge, and advocates for27
a shift in the consideration of the tropical cyclone impact from im mediate damage to eﬀects28
on long-term natural recovery cycles.29
1 Introduction30
Species composing ecosystems have co-evolved with the geo- climatic conditions at their location. Hence,31
they are not only adapted to their average climatic conditions such as the temperature or precipitation32
regimes, but also to extreme events such as ﬂoods, wildﬁres, or t ropical cyclones. Climate change is rapidly33
modifying the climatic conditions which has been shown to lea d to a shift in the optimal climatic envelope34

compositions and lead to profound modiﬁcations of their functi oning. In addition, climate change is rapidly36
modifying the regional and temporal patterns of extreme events [ 2], which might further impact global37
ecosystems. An increase in frequency and intensity of extreme we ather events can even promote more rapid38
and durable shifts to ecosystems than the gradual increase of the means of climate values [3–6].39
Climate change is expected to modify the intensity and frequenc y of tropical cyclones and their ge-40
ographical impacts [7–9]. Tropical cyclones are intense low-p ressure systems that form over warm ocean41
waters which can produce strong winds, heavy rainfall, and storm su rges. As the climate warms, the surface42
waters of the oceans become warmer, providing more fuel for tropical s torms to grow in strength. Further,43
climate change is expected to increase the amount of moisture i n the atmosphere, which can lead to more44
intense rainfall during tropical storms, and the rising sea level w ill expose new areas to storm surges.45
Flooding and landslides from the heavy rainfalls, strong winds, a nd storm surges can cause wide-scale dis-46
turbances for the ecosystem in the path of the storm and beyond [1 0, 11]. This may aﬀect all dimensions,47
including the ecosystem’s biogeochemistry, hydrography, mob ile biota, sedentary biota, or micro-biota [5,48
12]. Beyond a change in cyclone severity, regional patterns are l ikely to change, thus exposing areas that49
have not historically experienced these types of storms [8, 13, 14], yielding unprecedented consequences for50
not only human societies [9, 15] but also for natural ecosystem s [12, 16, 17].51
Tropical cyclones have been shown to impact ecosystems throug h various processes such as wind throw,52
causing defoliation and canopy damage, which can lead to reduc ed photosynthesis, growth, and produc-53
tivity of trees, as well as increased susceptibility to pest an d disease outbreaks [16, 18]. Heavy rain can54
cause landslides, soil erosion, and nutrient loss [19] potent ially causing decreased growth and productivity55
of vegetation, but also direct vegetation mortality [20], esp ecially when already disturbed by anthropogenic56
land use [21]. Storms can add large amounts of saltwater to coas tal freshwater and terrestrial ecosystems57
disrupting habitats [22]. Together, disturbances from tropical s torms can alter the structure and compo-58
sition of ecosystems, change the habitat quality for animal s pecies, and lead to mortality or displacement59
of biota [23, 24], causing local loss of biodiversity [25, 26] . For instance, extreme weather events account60
for 11% of global mangrove forest loss between 2000 - 2020 [27]. Pre vious methods, such as remote sensing61
and ﬁeld studies have sought to quantify the immediate impact of tropical storms on forests [28], but not62
how they may modify ecosystems over the long run. Several studie s on the eﬀect of storms on ecosystems63
have been conducted, most focused on explicit relation for spec iﬁc species [29], part of the vegetation [17,64
30], or for regional ecosystems [12]. However, estimating the p otential eﬀect of tropical cyclones on global65
ecosystems with a long time perspective is limited by methodo logical challenges [17, 31–35].66
While tropical cyclones may appear as purely destructive extrem e events, they also generate regular67
disturbances that are naturally structuring the ecosystems’ dyn amics [36]. The impacts of tropical cyclones68
are immediately followed by recovery and therefore those ecosyste ms are in a state of dynamic equilibrium69
[12, 16, 17, 31, 37]. Furthermore, tropical cyclones may support t he regulation of certain ecological pro-70
cesses such as water provision, soil enrichment with nutrients , restoration of ecological niches, support seed71
dispersion, or temporarily mixing the salinity and phytoplank ton content in estuaries [38]. Thus, as long72
as the ecosystem is resilient, tropical cyclones can also play a functional role, shaping speciﬁc ecosystems73
[31, 39, 40] and being a necessary perturbation to maintain thei r functionality, thus supporting biodiversity74
following the intermediate disturbance hypothesis [41–45] .75
Coastal ecosystems are known to provide a large number of essent ial services to Human societies such as76
nutrient cycling, erosion control, hatchery for ﬁshes and crustac eans, water puriﬁcation, cultural importance,77
tourism, carbon sequestration, and circularly, protection from cyc lones [46–48]. Thus, it is timely to assess78
the risk of tropical cyclones under climate change to ecosyste ms [49] and integrate this knowledge into the79
socio-economic risk assessment methodologies established in the context of the Intergovernmental Panel on80
Climate Change (IPCC) [2] and Sendai Disaster Risk Reduction [50] frameworks. We propose here using81
the open-source CLIMADA risk modelling platform [51] to investi gate the vulnerability of global coastal82
ecosystems to tropical cyclones at a global scale based on the panarchy principles [52]. More precisely, we83

middle, and high) as being either vulnerable, resilient, or de pendent [56] (c.f., Fig. 1) based on the panarchy115

exposed to the disturbance and the damages of tropical cyclone s are high since it has likely not evolved117
resilience and recovery mechanisms. In contrast, a resilient eco system has historically frequently been118
exposed to tropical cyclone disturbance and thus has evolved t o cope with the destructive eﬀects and119
