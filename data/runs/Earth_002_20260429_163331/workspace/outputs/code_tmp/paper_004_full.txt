Abstract12
Coastal ecosystems are recognized to provide a large range of servi ces including erosion13
prevention, clean water provision, or protection from tropical cy clones storms. However, with14
climate change, the increase in frequency and intensity of trop ical cyclones may alter the com-15
position of the ecosystems themselves potentially degradin g the various services they provide.16
While in general ecosystems are adapted to external perturbation s, extreme events can pro-17
mote rapid and durable shifts. In this study, we quantify the vuln erability of all ecosystems18
described as ecoregions at a global scale by analyzing the wind ﬁelds of tropical cyclones gen-19
erated from probabilistic historical tracks. We then investigate how tropical cyclone intensity20
and frequency shifting in a warming climate might lead to ecosyst em modiﬁcations. We show21
that a combined 13% of the surface of all terrestrial ecosystems is susceptible to transformation22
due to cyclone pattern changes between 2020 and 2050 under curre nt policies. Even for the23
most resilient ecosystems already experiencing winds above 60 m/s regularly, the average inter-24
val between two storms is projected to decrease from 19 to 12 years whi ch is potentially close25
to their recovery time. Our global probabilistic approach being embedded in the established26
IPCC climate risk frameworks enables the study of a broad temporal ra nge, and advocates for27
a shift in the consideration of the tropical cyclone impact from im mediate damage to eﬀects28
on long-term natural recovery cycles.29
1 Introduction30
Species composing ecosystems have co-evolved with the geo- climatic conditions at their location. Hence,31
they are not only adapted to their average climatic conditions such as the temperature or precipitation32
regimes, but also to extreme events such as ﬂoods, wildﬁres, or t ropical cyclones. Climate change is rapidly33
modifying the climatic conditions which has been shown to lea d to a shift in the optimal climatic envelope34
1
for a large number of species among insects, vertebrates, or plan ts [1]. This may alter larger ecosystems’35
compositions and lead to profound modiﬁcations of their functi oning. In addition, climate change is rapidly36
modifying the regional and temporal patterns of extreme events [ 2], which might further impact global37
ecosystems. An increase in frequency and intensity of extreme we ather events can even promote more rapid38
and durable shifts to ecosystems than the gradual increase of the means of climate values [3–6].39
Climate change is expected to modify the intensity and frequenc y of tropical cyclones and their ge-40
ographical impacts [7–9]. Tropical cyclones are intense low-p ressure systems that form over warm ocean41
waters which can produce strong winds, heavy rainfall, and storm su rges. As the climate warms, the surface42
waters of the oceans become warmer, providing more fuel for tropical s torms to grow in strength. Further,43
climate change is expected to increase the amount of moisture i n the atmosphere, which can lead to more44
intense rainfall during tropical storms, and the rising sea level w ill expose new areas to storm surges.45
Flooding and landslides from the heavy rainfalls, strong winds, a nd storm surges can cause wide-scale dis-46
turbances for the ecosystem in the path of the storm and beyond [1 0, 11]. This may aﬀect all dimensions,47
including the ecosystem’s biogeochemistry, hydrography, mob ile biota, sedentary biota, or micro-biota [5,48
12]. Beyond a change in cyclone severity, regional patterns are l ikely to change, thus exposing areas that49
have not historically experienced these types of storms [8, 13, 14], yielding unprecedented consequences for50
not only human societies [9, 15] but also for natural ecosystem s [12, 16, 17].51
Tropical cyclones have been shown to impact ecosystems throug h various processes such as wind throw,52
causing defoliation and canopy damage, which can lead to reduc ed photosynthesis, growth, and produc-53
tivity of trees, as well as increased susceptibility to pest an d disease outbreaks [16, 18]. Heavy rain can54
cause landslides, soil erosion, and nutrient loss [19] potent ially causing decreased growth and productivity55
of vegetation, but also direct vegetation mortality [20], esp ecially when already disturbed by anthropogenic56
land use [21]. Storms can add large amounts of saltwater to coas tal freshwater and terrestrial ecosystems57
disrupting habitats [22]. Together, disturbances from tropical s torms can alter the structure and compo-58
sition of ecosystems, change the habitat quality for animal s pecies, and lead to mortality or displacement59
of biota [23, 24], causing local loss of biodiversity [25, 26] . For instance, extreme weather events account60
for 11% of global mangrove forest loss between 2000 - 2020 [27]. Pre vious methods, such as remote sensing61
and ﬁeld studies have sought to quantify the immediate impact of tropical storms on forests [28], but not62
how they may modify ecosystems over the long run. Several studie s on the eﬀect of storms on ecosystems63
have been conducted, most focused on explicit relation for spec iﬁc species [29], part of the vegetation [17,64
30], or for regional ecosystems [12]. However, estimating the p otential eﬀect of tropical cyclones on global65
ecosystems with a long time perspective is limited by methodo logical challenges [17, 31–35].66
While tropical cyclones may appear as purely destructive extrem e events, they also generate regular67
disturbances that are naturally structuring the ecosystems’ dyn amics [36]. The impacts of tropical cyclones68
are immediately followed by recovery and therefore those ecosyste ms are in a state of dynamic equilibrium69
[12, 16, 17, 31, 37]. Furthermore, tropical cyclones may support t he regulation of certain ecological pro-70
cesses such as water provision, soil enrichment with nutrients , restoration of ecological niches, support seed71
dispersion, or temporarily mixing the salinity and phytoplank ton content in estuaries [38]. Thus, as long72
as the ecosystem is resilient, tropical cyclones can also play a functional role, shaping speciﬁc ecosystems73
[31, 39, 40] and being a necessary perturbation to maintain thei r functionality, thus supporting biodiversity74
following the intermediate disturbance hypothesis [41–45] .75
Coastal ecosystems are known to provide a large number of essent ial services to Human societies such as76
nutrient cycling, erosion control, hatchery for ﬁshes and crustac eans, water puriﬁcation, cultural importance,77
tourism, carbon sequestration, and circularly, protection from cyc lones [46–48]. Thus, it is timely to assess78
the risk of tropical cyclones under climate change to ecosyste ms [49] and integrate this knowledge into the79
socio-economic risk assessment methodologies established in the context of the Intergovernmental Panel on80
Climate Change (IPCC) [2] and Sendai Disaster Risk Reduction [50] frameworks. We propose here using81
the open-source CLIMADA risk modelling platform [51] to investi gate the vulnerability of global coastal82
ecosystems to tropical cyclones at a global scale based on the panarchy principles [52]. More precisely, we83
will study the spatial frequency distributions of the storms und er current climatic conditions (see illustration84

3 Data and Methods175
3.1 Tropical cyclones176
Tropical cyclones are described as a probabilistic set of wind ﬁe lds of maximum 1-minute sustained winds177
at 10 meters above the ground. Thus, in this study, we consider t he eﬀect of tropical cyclone winds on178
ecosystems and do only implicitly consider the precipitatio ns, surges, landslides, or ﬂoods that often are179
caused by tropical cyclones. The wind ﬁelds are computed using the Holland model [58] from a probabilistic180
tropical cyclone tracks (time series of the position, pressure, a nd maximum wind speed of the center of the181
cyclones) as implemented in the risk modeling platform CLIMADA [51] . The probabilistic set of tracks182
is obtained from the STORM model [53], a statistical model base d on the observational data from the183
IBTrACS [59] database (c.f. Fig. 2). As the baseline we conside r a set of 10 , 000 years of simulated tropical184
cyclones for climate conditions representative for the period 19 80 − 2018 [53] (for a total of 144 , 000 tracks185
worldwide). We also consider 10 , 000 years of simulated tropical cyclones for climate condition s in 2050186
under the emission scenario RCP8 .5 [13]. In both cases, each storm in the 10 , 000 year set is thus assigned187
an annual occurrence probability of 1 /10, 000. For the future scenario, we consider the outputs from four188
global circulation models (i.e. four times 10 , 000 years of data). All the metrics reported in the results189
section are then the median over all four models.190
Since maximum sustained wind speed from tropical cyclones can ra nge from 20m/s to 100m/s or more,191
their destructive power can vary a lot. Thus, we divide the tropi cal cyclone wind intensities into three192
categories low (L), middle (M), and high (H) based on the Saﬃr-S impson scale (SSHWS):193
• Low : 33 − 49m/s - SSHWS 1-2194
• Middle: 49 − 58m/s - SSHWS 3195
• High: ≥ 58 m/s - SSHWS 4-5196
These correspond to the percentiles 72, 93, and 98 of the used STO RM dataset wind speeds for the current197
climate (see Supplementary Fig. 2). For each intensity categ ory, at each grid location (global resolution of198
150arcsec on land), we then obtain the average annual occurrence probability (frequency), or equivalently199
its inverse, the average return period. Note that there are between 1 00 − 150 storms per year on the planet.200
The average return periods for each intensity category for the basel ine climate are shown in Supplemen-201
tary Fig. 1. We do not consider return periods larger than 250 years h ere. We assume that ecosystems, and202
in particular other environmental factors such as temperature, hu midity, or precipitation, as well as Human203
factors such as urbanization, change on timescales much faster t han would be required to be in dynamical204
equilibrium with events that occur less than with 0 .4% probability, i.e. with a return period of 250 years.205
3.2 Vulnerability of ecosystems206
It is assumed that an ecosystem must be in a stable equilibrium with the external perturbations acting on207
it. To describe the vulnerability of ecosystems to tropical cyc lones, we thus use a purely statistical approach208
based on the description of the tropical cyclone’s temporal pat terns. This approach is strongly inspired by209
the well-established framework for extreme weather risk analysis [2, 51].210
We base the logic of the classiﬁcation on the following argumen ts. An ecosystem is vulnerable to211
tropical cyclone winds of a given intensity if it has rarely been exposed to disturbance and thus when it212
is, the damages have potentially strong consequences and mig ht strongly modify the ecosystem which has213
not evolved resilience and recovery mechanism. A resilient ecos ystem has frequently been exposed to the214
disturbance and thus has evolved to recover and cope with the ne gative eﬀects on time scales of at least the215
average disturbance frequency. A dependent ecosystem is experie ncing regular tropical cyclones on almost216
the entirety of its territory. Or, conversely, the ecosystems exis t only there where there are regular tropical217
cyclones passing. Hence, it is likely that tropical cyclones play a key role in maintaining the ecosystems in218
their current state.219

4 Discussion250
Contrasting with many local assessments [21, 26] associated with speciﬁc events [25], here we derived poten-251
tial vulnerability relationships between the large-scale nat ural hazards of tropical cyclones, and ecosystems252
at a global scale. We used our approach inspired by the panarchy p rinciples [52, 60] and based on the253
frequency patterns of the storms to characterize ecosystem vulnera bility under present climate conditions.254
We identiﬁed ecosystems susceptible to being exposed to tran sformative pressure from tropical cyclone pat-255
tern changes due to a warming climate as early as 2050. Thus, we ex panded the study of extreme climate256
risks from the extensively studied socio-economic realm [2, 61– 65] to include ecosystems complementing a257
growing body of research on the topic [17, 49, 66]. We found that cl imate change might signiﬁcantly aﬀect258
a large number of coastal ecosystems through tropical cyclone p atterns change. Most ecosystems currently259
exposed to tropical cyclones will be confronted with an increase in their frequency and intensity thus nar-260
rowing the maximum boundary for their recovery. This might have se vere consequences for the ability of261
these ecosystems to prevail under future climatic conditions a nd is likely to result in further biodiversity262
losses [67] as is accounted for in climatic envelop models whi ch do not fully account for extreme weather263
pattern changes [1, 68, 69]. Furthermore, this might reshape cons iderations for the viability of coastal264
ecosystem-based adaptation measures to climate change [46, 70, 71], since the ecosystems themselves are265
at risk of being transformed. While previous studies have done loc al or ecosystem-speciﬁc assessments or266
projections [12, 16, 30], our study oﬀers a global assessment of ecosystem disturbances by tropical cyclones267
under climate change.268
The change in geographical distribution of cyclones may result in several new ecosystems being exposed,269
in particular further away from the tropics with an increase in storms undergoing extra-tropical transitions270
[72–75]. Forests and other coastal ecosystems at higher lati tudes are not resilient to tropical cyclones since,271
in contrast to low latitude areas, no evolutionary forces made the m able to withstand those perturbations272
[12]. Among the potentially newly aﬀected ecoregions, some a re located far outside of the tropics, where273
recent tropical storms have already strongly impacted ecosystem s [39]. For those regions historically hit by274
storms, the general projected increase in storm frequency and intens ity might cause particularly important275
changes to ecosystems as illustrated by the recent events Ana i n Mozambique in 2022 [76] or Amphan in276
Bangladesh in 2020 [77, 78]. A few ecosystems might in contrast have to deal with a signiﬁcant decrease277
in frequency, which also could lead to a change in composition d ue to the disappearance of a functional278
disturbance. This might change the natural ecosystems’ dynam ics and may inﬂuence their associated279
biodiversity [67].280
The major strengths of our approach are the global applicability , the exclusive use of available open-281
data sources, the possibility to extrapolate the results to futu re climates, the computational simplicity, the282
parametric ﬂexibility, the purely statistical basis of the app roach, the potential for direct generalization283
to other climate hazards, and the seamless integration into th e established IPCC risk [2, 79] framework.284
While we here concentrated on the characterization of vulnerabil ities and risk under future climates, the285
approach also provides estimates of upper bounds to recovery tim es from the average return periods of286
the storms (c.f. Supplementary Table 1). These can potentially inform dynamical ecosystem models [35],287
or help ecosystem management practitioners anticipate poten tial regime changes. Furthermore, since the288
proposed framework is seamlessly integrated into the open-source risk modeling platform CLIMADA [80],289
it is easily applicable to any natural hazard, such as ﬂoods, ea rthquakes, extreme heat, or wildﬁres. For290
several of these hazards, open data is readily available from the I SIMIP [81] or the CLIMADA data APIs291
[80]. Note however that for some hazards the assessment might b e more involved since the intensity and292
frequency might depend on the ecosystem itself – think for insta nce of wildﬁres whose ignition probability293
depends on the available biomass fuel. This challenge could b e tackled by qualifying ecosystems not only294
as resilient, vulnerable, or dependent but also as drivers of the hazard.295
Our study represents one step towards a systemic, global, and lo ng-term understanding of ecosystems’296
vulnerability to tropical cyclones. It has some limitations t hat open perspectives for more detailed quan-297
tiﬁcation in the future. The proposed statistical approach base d on the hazard distribution alone does298
10
not contain any ecological function understanding as this is n ot yet feasible at the global scale [82]. Our299
approach hinges on the assumption that ecosystems have co-ev olved with the extreme disturbances at their300
location. However, given the major anthropogenic pressure on all planetary ecosystems in the last centuries301
[83, 84], it is from the beginning only partially valid to assume ecosystem co-evolution with tropical cy-302
clone disturbances. Furthermore, the fact that tropical cyclone a ctivity has ﬂuctuated over time inherently303
requires making an arbitrary choice on the relevant timescales. We focused on timescales relevant to the304
current rapidly changing climate. In addition, several semi-in formed, partially normative, parametrizations305
choices had to be made, which may aﬀect the outcomes: the deﬁn ition of the storm intensity categories,306
the choice of spatial extent for the vulnerability deﬁnitions , the choice of return period maxima for the re-307
silient and dependent ecosystems, as well as the thresholds fo r the signiﬁcant changes in a changing climate.308
Furthermore, we only considered the changes in the annual frequenc y distributions. Thus, we did neither309
consider the potential eﬀects on ecosystems from a change in th e seasonality of tropical cyclones [85], nor310
do we account for the clustering in time properties, which may foll ow multi-year patterns in several regions311
[86]. We also did only implicitly consider the resulting storm surges, heavy precipitations, landslides, or312
ﬂoods which would be explicitly needed for a mechanistic unde rstanding of the impacts of tropical cyclones313
on coastal ecosystems. We further did not consider similar stro ng wind hazards such as winter storms,314
gales, or Derechos that might co-aﬀect certain ecosystems. Fin ally, our study is bound by the limitations315
of the STORM model itself in representing current and future tropica l cyclone activity [9, 13, 87], and is316
bound to the speciﬁc ecoregion boundaries deﬁned by Dinerstein et al. [55].317
In conclusion, our study shows how changes in tropical cyclon e patterns under a changing climate can318
impact a large number of coastal ecosystems by the year 2050. T he identiﬁed potential strong impact of319
tropical cyclone pattern changes alone indicates the need for t he more explicit inclusion of the eﬀect of320
extreme weather hazards on global ecosystems in earth-system m odels to improve our understanding of321
climate projections [82, 88]. In the context of ecosystem mana gement to mitigate and adapt to climate322
change, our ﬁndings show that we need to not only consider imm ediate damages from a tropical cyclone323
event but also think about multi-year shifts in ecosystem traje ctories in a changing climate. This includes324
the need to better assess and report the consequences of storms fo r ecosystems at a global scale. Our study325
does not directly allow us to conclude whether the increased ris k to ecosystems translates into a decrease326
or an increase in ecosystem service provisions, yet based on eco system disturbance studies, a strongly327
perturbed ecosystem is expected to be less capable of sustaini ng biodiversity, carbon storage or providing328
ﬂood protection [89]. Considering the further pressure on coastal ecosystems from both average climate329
variables changes [1] and anthropogenic activities [83], it i s worrying to add yet another potential threat to330
the list in the form of tropical cyclones.331
